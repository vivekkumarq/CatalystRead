Being a good candidate is a skill. Being a good **interviewer** is a different one: you hold a **rubric**, you sample **work**, you take notes that another person could use, and you do not decide in the first two minutes. Unstructured "chat about Kubernetes" hires people who chat. Structured loops still mis-hire, but they fail in ways you can debug.

## Before the call

Know the level and the **signals** (debugging, design, collaboration, domain). Align with the panel so you do not all ask the same system-design question. Send a candidate a realistic agenda. Surprise extra hours of take-home without pay is a filter for desperation, not talent.

A good problem is a **slice of the job**: a service that times out, a schema that cannot migrate, a PR with a subtle race. Puzzle questions (brain teasers) measure puzzle history.

## During

Talk less than you think. Let them think. If they go silent, wait. Hints should be **laddered** and noted (solved with hint vs without). Do not interview from the answer key in a way that punishes equivalent designs. For behavioral: **STAR** is a note-taking aid, not a ritual humiliation. Probe **their** decisions, not the team's brand names.

Write evidence: "Proposed outbox for dual-write, named the failure if consumer is not idempotent." Not: "Seemed strong."

## After

Score against the rubric **before** the debrief so the loudest staff engineer does not rewrite everyone's memory. A "no" needs a dimension. A "yes" needs risks (needs mentoring in X). Shadow a calibrated interviewer. If your yes-rate is 90% or 5%, you are not using the same bar as the panel.

Legal and ethics: avoid questions that elicit protected information. Stick to the job.

Read a structured hiring chapter (Smart/Street or re:Work). Then rewrite one question you always ask so it has a rubric of 1–4 with examples. The loop is a measurement instrument. If you cannot say what you measured, you measured confidence. Confidence is not a level.
