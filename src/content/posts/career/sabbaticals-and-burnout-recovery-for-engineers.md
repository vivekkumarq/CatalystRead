Burnout is an **occupational** phenomenon (WHO): exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy. It is not a personal failure to "hack sleep." Engineers burn out on on-call that never ends, moral injury (shipping what they don't believe), and identity fused to output. A **sabbatical** (unpaid or paid, policy-dependent) is a long protected absence. A long weekend is not a sabbatical. Recovery has a **timeline** and a **coverage plan**, like an incident.

## Before you leave

Write down ownership: systems, upcoming dates, who is backup. If nothing can be handed off, the org is the problem; leaving anyway may still be right. HR and legal: know the policy, visa implications, health insurance. Do not "quietly" work from the beach; that is neither rest nor honest employment.

Handoff docs beat tribal knowledge. Freeze non-essential projects. The team should feel a **planned** load, not a sudden hole that creates a second burnout.

## During

Medical advice is individual; this is not a treatment plan. In general: reduce inputs (Slack off), restore sleep, move the body, see people who are not coworkers. Nagoski's "complete the stress cycle" is a useful frame: rest is not only absence of tickets. If you are in crisis, use professional and local emergency resources — a memo cannot substitute.

Do not build a startup in the first month of a burnout leave unless a clinician and you agree you have capacity. Many people mistake mania for recovery.

## Return

Ramp: no on-call week one if you can negotiate it. A "welcome back" pile of 400 threads is how relapse starts. Manager: protect a **scope diet**. If the causes (staffing, abuse, impossible goals) are unchanged, leave again — including leaving the company. Sabbatical is not a loyalty test.

Read the WHO note and, if it fits, Nagoski for the physiology of stress. Then, if you manage people, put **coverage** in the team's operating system so leave is possible. A culture that cannot survive a three-month absence was already one resignation from failure. Recovery is infrastructure. Treat it like you treat a datacenter: redundant humans, not heroes.
