**Managing up** is keeping the person who owns your scope **able to decide**. It is not flattery, and it is not a shadow org. Politics is when information is used as currency. The alternative is a **cadence**: what moved, what is blocked, what you need from them, and **what will blow up if we wait**. Grove's "task-relevant maturity" still applies: more detail when they are new to the domain, less when they trust the system.

## The no-surprise rule

If a date will slip, they hear it from you with a new date and a cut list, not from a dashboard in a QBR. If you disagree with a direction, you say so **with an option**, not with a leak to their peers first. Escalation is a tool: use it when the decision is above your pay band, and tell your manager you are doing it.

```text
This week: canary 20%, error rate 0.4% → 0.2%
Risk: vendor SLA miss on Friday; need approval to fail open
Ask: 30 min with security on the exception
```

Written beats hallway for anything that will be repeated. A short doc scales to skip-levels.

## Options, not just problems

"We need more people" is not a decision. "Drop feature B, slip two weeks, or pull Jane for 50%" is a decision. Staff engineers who only bring problems train managers to treat them as weather.

Don't optimize for looking busy. Optimize for **decision quality**. If they choose against your recommendation, execute or leave — lingering sabotage is politics.

## Boundaries

You are not their therapist or their spokesperson in every slack war. Protect maker time; batch updates. If they want daily stand-up theater, propose a written pulse that takes five minutes.

Read Grove on meetings and Fournier on managing up chapters. Then replace one status meeting with a doc for two weeks. If your manager is more informed and you wrote less theater, you managed up. If you spent the week crafting a narrative that hid the risk, you did the other thing. The difference is whether they can still steer.
